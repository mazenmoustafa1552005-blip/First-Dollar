### A2. What buyers praise (pattern across rows)

1. **Kids physically hunting for clues and a final treasure/reveal** (rows 9, 15).
2. **Well-organized kit and simple setup** (rows 9, 10).
3. **Difficulty pitched right for first-timers** ("good entry-level", rows 7, 10).
4. **Strong host support**: a one-page game overview and hints the host can give (rows 14, 15).
5. **Everyone could participate**, including a wide age span (row 15).

### A3. What buyers complain about

1. **Too easy / solved early** (row 6: solved before round 2) and, elsewhere, **too hard** (Diamond Heist reviewer: younger girls could not figure out 10-12 level clues; escape-kit.com guide: clues "too easy (solved in 10 seconds) or too hard", see market-evidence.md).
2. **Ran longer than stated** (row 2; row 6 ran about 2 hours; row 13 suggests 2-3 hours).
3. **Too much reading or writing for kids** (rows 8, 9, 14).
4. **Host could not verify answers** because solutions were not on the host sheet (row 14).
5. **Prep load: printing and cutting** (row 14; Room Escape Artist kit reviews in market-evidence.md).
6. **Age-band confusion** (row 6 listings disagree; market spans 5-9, 7-10, 7-12, 8-13, 9+, 10+).
7. **Role games need a minimum headcount** (8+ suspects) and are hard to fit small families (rows 6, 13).
8. **Licence surprises** for classroom/paid events (row 8).

### A4. Copy / avoid

| Copy | Avoid |
|---|---|
| Detective format (scales from 3 to 15 kids; no minimum cast) | Role-play formats that need a fixed number of guests |
| Non-murder, "no crime" or light-theft framing | Murder, kidnapping, realistic danger |
| Host one-page overview with **all answers on it** | Solutions hidden only in a separate booklet |
| Squads of 2-5 with plans for small and large groups (row 3) | One-size player count |
| Two difficulty levels (seen on 6-10 products, not on 8-12 detective games) | A single difficulty for the whole 8-12 band |
| Sealed rounds and a final treasure/treat | Writing out clues by hand (row 9) |
| Host-delivered tiered hints (row 15) | Single-path ciphers with no hint |
| Explicit home vs classroom licence | Surprise licence terms |
| Stated, tested play time and setup time | Vendor-estimated times that run long |

## B. Psychology of the players (children 8-12)

Each block: Finding (sourced) then Rule (ours).

### B1. Reasoning and deduction: 8-9 vs 11-12

**Findings**
- Piaget's concrete operational stage is placed at about 7-11: children reason logically about tangible things but have difficulty with "what if" hypotheticals; hypothetical-deductive reasoning (form a hypothesis, test it systematically) defines the formal operational stage, which sources start at 11 or 12. Sources also note stages are contested and development is more continuous (Lumen Learning intro psychology texts).
- Control-of-variables reasoning (a scientific-reasoning skill close to "test one suspect at a time"): gains between ages 8 and 12, with 9-11 described as a critical period; children about 8 can prefer conclusive over confounded comparisons but do not use the strategy consistently; transfer to new problems starts around age 10 (Peteranderl and Edelsbrunner 2020, Frontiers in Psychology; Bramley et al. review; Chen and Klahr 1999).
- Second-order theory of mind ("he thinks that she thinks") becomes reliable around 6-7 on standard tasks; it correlates with detecting lies, and 7-9 year olds' second-order ToM correlates with understanding a liar's intention (Talwar, Gordon and Lee 2007 and related studies).

**Rules**
- Every clue states a **concrete, checkable fact** (a shoe size, a time, a colour, a crumb), never an abstract motive inference.
- The core solution path is **elimination**: each evidence card lets players cross out at least one suspect on a printed grid.
- **Junior tier (8-9):** each card eliminates directly ("the thief had muddy shoes; X's shoes are clean").
- **Expert tier (10-12):** at least two eliminations require **combining two cards**, and one suspect statement contains a single lie that a card exposes.
- Suspect statements in the Junior tier are truthful but incomplete; no lies to detect.

### B2. Working memory: how many suspects and clues

**Findings**
- Young adults hold about 3-5 chunks; capacity grows across childhood. Estimates for 7-year-olds range from about 1.5 to 3 items depending on method; 7-year-olds accessed fewer clauses than 12-year-olds or adults (Cowan and colleagues, 2010 Developmental Psychology and related work).
- Working-memory span grows roughly linearly from 4 to 15 (Gathercole, Pickering, Ambridge and Waring 2004). 8-10 year olds outscore 4-6 year olds on complex span (Buttelmann et al. 2020).
- Children with low working memory "forget what they have to do next" and fail multi-step instructions; recommended fixes: less material, shorter sentences, fewer steps per instruction, diagrams and colour, break tasks into parts (Gathercole and Alloway classroom guidance, summarized in Oxfordshire and Swindon council guides; Alloway 2006 review).
- Split-attention: learning suffers when learners must integrate separate mutually-referring sources; integrate labels next to what they refer to; colour coding helps (Chandler and Sweller 1992; Kalyuga, Chandler and Sweller 1999).

**Rules**
- **Max 5 suspects** in Junior, **max 6** in Expert. (Our judgement: with an elimination grid the grid holds the state, so the child only holds the current card plus one fact.)
- **Max 3 tracked attributes per suspect** (for example: where, wearing, holding).
- **Max 4 evidence cards per round**, **max 12 in the game**.
- **Never require holding more than 2 facts in mind at once**: the grid externalizes memory; the Expert "combine two cards" step uses side-by-side cards.
- Each evidence card shows the **suspect portrait icon(s) it concerns** next to the text (avoids split attention).
- Host instructions: **one action per numbered step**, max 3 steps per round-start script.

### B3. Reading level and text length

**Findings**
- Oral reading fluency, 50th percentile words correct per minute (Hasbrouck and Tindal 2017 norms): grade 3 fall 83 / spring 112; grade 4 fall 94 / spring 133; grade 5 fall 121 / spring 146 (grade 5 row from one secondary source); grade 6 spring 146 with 10th percentile about 91. Ages 8-9 are typically grades 3-4; ages 11-12 grades 6-7.
- Vendors note reading ability varies widely at the same age; kits flag that younger guests need adult help reading clues (Night of Mystery, My Mystery Party Bug City); a kids kit got harder because players had to sort through a lot of text (Room Escape Artist, Great Wizard Escape).

**Rules** (word limits are our derivation: a grade-3 fall reader at the 25th percentile is well below 83 WCPM, so we target a card readable aloud in about 30 seconds by a slow 8-year-old)
- **Evidence card: max 35 words** (Junior), **max 50 words** (Expert).
- **Suspect card: max 50 words** plus portrait and 3 attribute icons.
- **Sentences max 12 words**; target Flesch-Kincaid grade **3 or below** (Junior) and **5 or below** (Expert); checked with a readability tool and logged.
- **No handwriting required** beyond ticking boxes or circling; never "write out the clue".
- Every round has a **non-reading job** (evidence keeper, map marker, magnifier) so pre-fluent readers contribute.

### B4. Frustration, hint timing and the "aha"

**Findings**
- The "assistance dilemma": withholding help can improve learning, too much help removes it; mid-level assistance beat both low and high levels in a chemistry tutor study; hints timed near a predicted 50% chance of success worked best in a programming tutor (Koedinger and Aleven 2007 and follow-ups; mostly older students and tutoring software, not children).
- Flow needs challenge-skill balance; a slight overload was more enjoyable than boredom (2016 CHI experiment; Baumann, Lurig and Engeser 2016).
- Insight ("aha") moments carry positive affect and improve memory for the problem and solution; the affective component appears to drive the memory advantage (Kizilirmak et al. 2016; Danek and Wiley 2020).
- Curiosity is a felt "information gap" (Loewenstein 1994); curiosity activates reward areas and improves recall (Kang et al. 2009, adults).
- Competitor evidence: kids lost interest when a game was too hard; groups solved too early when clues were too easy (A3).

**Rules**
- **Three hint tiers per round**: Nudge (where to look), Pointer (which two cards to compare), Reveal (which suspect to cross out and why).
- **Hint clock per 12-minute round**: host offers Nudge at **minute 4** if no suspect has been crossed out, Pointer at **minute 7**, Reveal at **minute 10**. Rounds end at minute 12 regardless. (Timings are our design choice; validate in playtest.)
- Each round ends with a **mini-reveal** (one suspect formally cleared, read aloud) so there are 3 "aha" beats before the final one.
- The opening hook poses one question kids care about within the **first 2 minutes** (information gap), and the final envelope stays sealed until the accusation.
- **Target in playtest:** at least half of teams cross out a suspect before the Pointer hint in each round; if fewer, the round is too hard; if every team finishes before minute 4, too easy.

### B5. Cooperative vs competitive play, fairness across ages

**Findings**
- Meta-analysis of 148 studies of 12-15 year olds: cooperative goal structures were associated with higher achievement and more positive peer relationships than competitive or individualistic ones (Roseth, Johnson and Johnson 2008, Psychological Bulletin). Applies to the top of our band only.
- Inequality aversion develops strongly between ages 3 and 8; most 7-8 year olds prefer allocations that remove inequality, and favour their own group (Fehr, Bernhard and Rockenbach 2008, Nature).

**Rules**
- **Default mode is cooperative**: one detective agency (small group) or teams of 2-4 that share the same evidence.
- Optional team race: teams compete only on **who submits the correct accusation first**, and **every team that solves gets the same badge**.
- **Mixed-age teams** with role cards (Reader, Recorder, Evidence Keeper, Lookout) that rotate each round.
- **Identical rewards for every child** (same badge, same treat); the final treasure is shared.
- No child is ever the culprit or accused (suspects are cards, not players).

### B6. Humor, suspense and what is too scary

**Findings**
- Humour stage "riddles and jokes (double meanings)" runs about 6-7 to 10-11; children can hold two meanings of a word, so puns and riddles land (McGhee 2002; Baker 2017 thesis).
- Children 8-12 are frightened more by realistic, plausible threats, especially violence to themselves or peers, and by events they believe could happen to them; older children are helped by reasoning-based reassurance (Cantor, University of Wisconsin).

**Rules**
- **No murder, injury, weapons, kidnapping, burglary of a family home, stranger danger or realistic threats** to children.
- Crime = a **silly theft or prank** with a sympathetic motive (wanted to surprise someone, was embarrassed, a pet did it partly).
- **At least one pun or wordplay per suspect** (name or catchphrase), one riddle per round.
- Suspense comes from **sealed envelopes, a countdown and "the trail goes cold"**, not from danger.
- Halloween titles use **cartoon spooky** (costumes, fake ghosts) and the "ghost" is always revealed as a person or explanation.
- Culprit confession ends with an **apology and a fix** (cake replaced, trophy returned).

### B7. Attention span and game length

**Findings**
- Sustained attention develops rapidly from 5-6 to 8-9, with a plateau from 8-9 to 11-12 (Betts et al. 2006, 57 children aged 5-12); in a study of 8, 10 and 12 year olds only the 8-year-olds showed a clear performance drop over time on the task.
- No source found supports a fixed "minutes per year of age" attention rule (gap).
- Competitor data: stated times of 45+ min and 60-90 min; reports of games running 2-3 hours and longer than stated (A1, A3).

**Rules**
- **Full game: 3 rounds x 12 minutes + intro and finale = 45-55 minutes.**
- **Quick version: 25-30 minutes** (2 rounds, 3 suspects removed in advance).
- **A movement step between rounds** (search the room for the next envelope).
- Stated play time in the listing = **median measured playtest time**, not an estimate.

### B8. Physical and tactile elements

**Findings**
- Concrete manipulatives showed moderate-to-large effects on retention in a meta-analysis of 55 maths studies (Carbonneau, Marley and Selig 2013); effects depend on how they are used. Evidence for party-game props specifically was not found (gap).
- Competitor reviews: kids loved hunting for clues and the final treasure (row 9); prep complaints concern cutting and printing (A3).

**Rules**
- **One sealed envelope per round** (Round 1, 2, 3, Final), labelled "Do not open until...".
- **At least one find-it action per round** (host hides the envelope; hiding spots listed in the host guide).
- A **final hidden treat or "treasure"** step, using items the host already has (stated in the guide as optional).
- **All cutting is host setup**, on straight lines only, total **max 15 minutes setup** (timed in a real test).
- Elimination grid and badges printable **B&W without loss of meaning** (icons and patterns, not colour alone).

### B9. Fair play

**Finding:** Knox's 1929 "Decalogue" rule 8 (fair play): the detective must not rely on clues not shown to the reader.

**Rule:** Every fact needed for the solution appears on a player-facing card; the solution is **unique**; every red herring is resolvable by a card; verified by an independent blind solve from buyer files only.

## C. Psychology of the buyer (parents and hosts)

### C1. Motivations and anxieties

**Findings**
- Kinder Joy / OnePoll survey (2,000 US parents of 3-12 year olds, Nov 2022): top stressors when planning a birthday party were having enough time to plan (55%) and keeping costs down (55%), then food (52%) and guest list (51%).
- PizzaExpress UK survey (1,500 parents): 74% found planning a child's party more stressful than their day job. A Netflix-commissioned survey reported an average 10 hours of planning (secondhand figure). CEC Entertainment 2026 study (1,878 US parents of 2-12 year olds) reports planning stress across income groups.
- Competitor complaints show the specific anxieties: will it fit my kids' ages and reading level, will it run long, will they solve it in 5 minutes, how much printing and cutting, can I check the answers, is it scary, can I use it at school (A3).

Motivations we infer from listings and occasions (not from a survey): a screen-free activity that fills the party's middle hour, a memorable "theme" without buying decorations, family night variety, classroom/club use.

### C2. Decision shortcuts

**Findings**
- Baymard Institute usability testing: 56% of users' first action on a product page was exploring the images; users confuse what is included with what is optional unless labelled; descriptive text on images helps judge suitability; customer photos raise confidence (2017-2019 studies, partly paywalled).
- Spiegel Research Center (Northwestern, with PowerReviews): purchase likelihood with five reviews was 270% greater than with none; marginal benefit falls after the first five; reviews matter more for higher-priced items.
- Etsy shoppers see listing review counts and shop-level ratings (rows 1-2), and competitor titles pack age, theme and player count into the title.

### C3. Ethical persuasion limits

- FTC final rule on fake reviews and testimonials (effective October 21, 2024): bans fake or AI-generated reviews, buying positive or negative reviews, undisclosed insider reviews and fake social-proof indicators, with civil penalties.
- Etsy's Anti-Shilling Policy prohibits fake reviews, asking others to write them and offering incentives for positive reviews (Etsy Seller Handbook).

### C4. Listing and product-page rules

1. **First image = the hook + three facts**: ages (with tier), players, play time.
2. **Second image = "Everything you get"**: thumbnails of every page, labelled with page count, colour vs B&W, and "optional, not included" for any household items.
3. **One real preview card** (a Round 1 evidence card) at readable size, checked at mobile width.
4. **Stated setup time and print load measured in a real test**: "N pages to print, about N minutes to cut on straight lines."
5. **Exact age fit**: "Junior (8-9) and Expert (10-12) clue sets included; readers needed: one per team."
6. **Group-size table**: 3-5, 6-10, 11-15 players and how teams split.
7. **Measured play time** with the quick-version option.
8. **Content reassurance line**: "No murder, no scary content: a missing [cake] and a silly culprit."
9. **Host confidence line**: "Answer key and hint script on one host page."
10. **Licence line**: home use included; classroom/club licence terms stated.
11. **Digital download notice**: instant download, no physical item, print at home or at a print shop, A4 and US Letter.
12. **Social proof only when real**: no reviews, testimonials or playtest quotes unless from actual buyers/testers with consent; no review incentives; playtest claims only after a logged real playtest.
13. **No guarantees about children's reactions** ("kids will love it guaranteed"); describe what is in the box and how it plays.
14. **Risk reduction that we can keep**: a stated contact path and a fix policy for file problems; "play the free 1-page sample case" lead magnet (common among competitors) to let parents test difficulty.
15. **Next purchase**: each listing names the sibling title or bundle (seasonal or second case).

## Design rules for First Dollar games (testable checklist)

Each rule must be checked per SKU and the evidence logged in the QA record.

1. Format is detective (all players investigate); no child plays the culprit or a suspect.
2. Crime is non-violent (theft/prank); no murder, injury, weapons, kidnapping, home burglary, stranger danger.
3. Culprit has a sympathetic motive and the confession ends with a fix.
4. Junior tier has max 5 suspects; Expert tier max 6.
5. Max 3 tracked attributes per suspect, shown as icons on the suspect card.
6. Max 4 evidence cards per round and max 12 per game.
7. Every evidence card states concrete, checkable facts and shows the icon(s) of the suspect(s) it concerns.
8. Every evidence card eliminates at least one suspect via the elimination grid.
9. Junior: each elimination needs one card. Expert: at least 2 eliminations need two cards combined, and exactly one suspect statement contains a card-exposable lie.
10. Evidence card text max 35 words (Junior) / 50 words (Expert); suspect card max 50 words; sentences max 12 words.
11. Readability: Junior at or below grade 3, Expert at or below grade 5 (tool output logged).
12. No handwriting required from players beyond ticks/circles.
13. Each round includes at least one non-reading role task.
14. Three rounds of 12 minutes; full game 45-55 minutes; quick version 25-30 minutes.
15. Three-tier hints per round with host cues at minutes 4, 7 and 10; hint script on the host page.
16. Each round ends with a mini-reveal clearing one suspect.
17. Opening hook states the mystery question within the first 2 minutes of the host script.
18. One sealed envelope per round plus a Final envelope; at least one find-it action per round.
19. Optional final treasure/treat step uses household items, labelled optional.
20. Default play is cooperative; team-race mode gives identical rewards to every solving team; role cards rotate each round.
21. At least one pun or wordplay per suspect and one riddle per round.
22. Host setup (print + cut) at most 15 minutes, straight cuts only, measured in a real test.
23. Host one-page overview includes the full solution, the clue-to-elimination map and the hint script.
24. Colour and B&W versions are both complete; no information is carried by colour alone.
25. A4 and US Letter exports both pass prepress checks.
26. Solution is unique and every red herring is resolvable from a card (fair play).
27. Independent blind solve from buyer files only succeeds before release.
